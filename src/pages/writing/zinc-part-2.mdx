## What problem are we solving?

[Part 1](/writing/zinc-part-1) built **Zinc-PIOP**: a polynomial interactive oracle proof over $\mathbb Q$ for integer-arithmetic relations. Sample a random prime $q$, project the witness through $\phi_q: \mathbb Z_{(q)} \to \mathbb F_q$, run a Spartan-shaped sum-check inside $\mathbb F_q$. Lemma 2.1 from Part 1 carried the whole soundness story — a bounded-bit-size witness can fool at most $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)$ primes, so random-prime sampling catches cheats.

A PIOP isn't a real argument though. To compile, we need a **polynomial commitment scheme** that:

- **Works over $\mathbb Q_B$**, not $\mathbb F_q$. The PIOP's witness oracle is multilinear with rational coefficients.
- **Extracts to bit-size-bounded rationals.** Lemma 2.1 only bites when the extracted witness has bounded entries. A generic $\mathbb Q$-PCS gives unbounded extraction, which kills soundness.
- **Doesn't use hidden-order groups.** The whole point of Zinc was to dodge the RSA-style assumptions CHA24 inherits.

That PCS is **Zip**. It's a Brakedown variant — Merkle-commit encoded rows of the coefficient matrix — built around a new primitive: the **IOP of Proximity to the Integers (IOPP-to-$\mathbb Z$)**. Plus a new linear code, **JEA** (Juxtaposed Expand-Accumulate), that survives the move from $\mathbb F_q$ to $\mathbb Q$ without bit-size blowup.

This part: Zip's contract, the commit/test/eval phases, the two combinatorial lemmas that drive its soundness, JEA codes, the extractor wrinkle, and how the pipeline compiles to a hash-only succinct argument via iCOS.

**Prerequisites.** Multilinear polynomials, the equality polynomial $\widetilde{eq}$, and the sum-check protocol (the [Spartan article](/writing/spartan-protocol) covers all three). Comfort with the projection $\phi_q$ and the localization $\mathbb Z_{(q)}$ from [Part 1](/writing/zinc-part-1). We build up Brakedown from scratch next, no prior exposure needed.

## Brakedown in a Nutshell

Zip is a Brakedown variant, so spend a minute on Brakedown first. Original setting: $\mathbb F_q$. Goal: commit to a multilinear $f(X_1, \ldots, X_\mu)$ with coefficients in $\mathbb F_q$, later prove $f(\mathbf q) = y$ for a query point $\mathbf q$ and value $y$.

### The tensor identity

This is the engine the whole scheme rides on, so let's derive it from scratch. A multilinear polynomial in $\mu$ variables is determined by its $2^\mu$ values on the boolean hypercube $\{0, 1\}^\mu$, via

$$f(\mathbf X) = \sum_{\mathbf b \in \{0, 1\}^\mu} f(\mathbf b) \cdot \widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b, \mathbf X),$$

where $\widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b, \mathbf X) = \prod_{i = 1}^{\mu} \big(b_i X_i + (1 - b_i)(1 - X_i)\big)$ is the multilinear extension of the equality indicator (the "selector" that's 1 when $\mathbf X = \mathbf b$ on the cube and 0 elsewhere — see [Spartan](/writing/spartan-protocol) for the underlying MLE machinery).

Now assume $\mu$ is even and split it as $\mu/2 + \mu/2$. Write $\mathbf b = (\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf b^{(2)})$ and $\mathbf X = (\mathbf X^{(1)}, \mathbf X^{(2)})$, each half of length $\mu/2$. Because $\widetilde{eq}$ is a product over coordinates, it factors:

$$\widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b, \mathbf X) = \widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf X^{(1)}) \cdot \widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b^{(2)}, \mathbf X^{(2)}).$$

Substituting:

$$f(\mathbf X) = \sum_{\mathbf b^{(1)}} \sum_{\mathbf b^{(2)}} f(\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf b^{(2)}) \cdot \widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf X^{(1)}) \cdot \widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b^{(2)}, \mathbf X^{(2)}).$$

Set $\dim = 2^{\mu/2}$ and define the **coefficient matrix** $\mathbf v^f \in \mathbb F_q^{\dim \times \dim}$ by $\mathbf v^f_{\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf b^{(2)}} = f(\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf b^{(2)})$, plus the **equality vectors**

$$\mathbf q_1 = \big(\widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b^{(1)}, \mathbf X^{(1)})\big)_{\mathbf b^{(1)} \in \{0,1\}^{\mu/2}}, \qquad \mathbf q_2 = \big(\widetilde{eq}(\mathbf b^{(2)}, \mathbf X^{(2)})\big)_{\mathbf b^{(2)} \in \{0,1\}^{\mu/2}}.$$

The double sum collapses to a quadratic form:

$$f(\mathbf X) = \mathbf q_1 \cdot \mathbf v^f \cdot \mathbf q_2^T.$$

That's the **tensor identity** — evaluating a multilinear at $\mathbf X = \mathbf q$ becomes "row-combine the matrix with $\mathbf q_1$, then column-combine with $\mathbf q_2$." Brakedown's commit phase commits to the matrix; the eval phase rides this identity.

(This is the form Brakedown uses verbatim, modulo notation. The Brakedown paper writes it as $g(\mathbf r) = \langle \mathbf q_1 \otimes \mathbf q_2, \mathbf u \rangle$ — same equation with the matrix flattened into a vector.)

**Worked example, carried through the section.** Let's pin down a concrete instance and reuse it across every phase. Take $\mathbb F_{11}$, $\mu = 2$, so $\dim = 2$ and $f$ has 4 coefficients on the hypercube $\{0, 1\}^2$. Pick

$$f(0,0) = 2, \quad f(0,1) = 3, \quad f(1,0) = 5, \quad f(1,1) = 7.$$

The coefficient matrix is

$$\mathbf v^f = \begin{pmatrix} f(0,0) & f(0,1) \\ f(1,0) & f(1,1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 5 & 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Pick the query point $\mathbf q = (3, 4)$. The equality vectors are

$$\mathbf q_1 = (\widetilde{eq}(0, 3),\, \widetilde{eq}(1, 3)) = (1 - 3,\, 3) = (-2, 3) \equiv (9, 3) \pmod{11},$$

$$\mathbf q_2 = (\widetilde{eq}(0, 4),\, \widetilde{eq}(1, 4)) = (1 - 4,\, 4) = (-3, 4) \equiv (8, 4) \pmod{11}.$$

The tensor identity says $f(3, 4) = \mathbf q_1 \cdot \mathbf v^f \cdot \mathbf q_2^T$. Step-by-step:

$$\mathbf q_1 \cdot \mathbf v^f = (9, 3) \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 5 & 7 \end{pmatrix} = (9 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 5,\; 9 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 7) = (33, 48) \equiv (0, 4) \pmod{11},$$

$$(0, 4) \cdot (8, 4)^T = 0 \cdot 8 + 4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}.$$

Sanity check by direct expansion: $f(3, 4) = 2 \cdot (1-3)(1-4) + 3 \cdot (1-3) \cdot 4 + 5 \cdot 3 \cdot (1-4) + 7 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 12 - 24 - 45 + 84 = 27 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$. ✓ The identity holds.

Hold on to $\mathbf v^f, \mathbf q_1, \mathbf q_2$. We'll keep using them.

### Commit phase

Pick a linear code $\mathcal C$ over $\mathbb F_q$ — a $\mathbb F_q$-subspace of $\mathbb F_q^{\mathsf n}$ of dimension $\dim$ with good minimum distance. Encode each row $\mathbf v_i^f$ separately via $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}: \mathbb F_q^{\dim} \to \mathbb F_q^{\mathsf n}$. Merkle-commit each encoded row. Output $\dim$ Merkle roots — that's the commitment.

**Worked example, continued.** For our running case ($\dim = 2$), pick the simplest rate-$1/2$ Reed–Solomon-style code with length $\mathsf n = 4$ that sends $(a, b) \mapsto (a, b, a + b, a + 2b)$. Generator matrix:

$$G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Encode each row of $\mathbf v^f$:

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\mathbf v_1^f) = (2, 3) \cdot G = (2,\; 3,\; 2 + 3,\; 2 + 6) = (2, 3, 5, 8) \pmod{11},$$

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\mathbf v_2^f) = (5, 7) \cdot G = (5,\; 7,\; 5 + 7,\; 5 + 14) = (5, 7, 1, 8) \pmod{11}.$$

The committed encoded matrix:

$$\hat{\mathbf u} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 5 & 8 \\ 5 & 7 & 1 & 8 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Merkle-commit each row. Output: two Merkle roots — one per row. That's the whole commitment. (Real Brakedown uses $|\mathbb F_q| \approx 2^{128}$ and far longer codewords, but the shape is identical.)

### Testing phase — "are these rows really codewords?"

The Merkle commitment binds the prover to _some_ word in $\mathbb F_q^{\mathsf n}$ per row, but nothing forces those words to be codewords. Testing checks that.

1. $\mathsf V$ samples random coefficients $r_1, \ldots, r_{\dim} \in \mathbb F_q$.
2. $\mathsf P$ sends $\bar{\mathbf v} = \sum_i r_i \cdot \mathbf v_i^f \in \mathbb F_q^{\dim}$ (the random linear combination of the underlying message rows).
3. $\mathsf V$ samples $J \subseteq [\mathsf n]$ with $\lvert J \rvert = \Theta(\lambda)$. For each $j \in J$, $\mathsf V$ opens column $j$ of every committed row (getting $\hat u_{1,j}, \ldots, \hat u_{\dim, j}$) and checks

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\bar{\mathbf v})_j = \sum_i r_i \cdot \hat u_{i,j}.$$

**Worked example, continued.** Say $\mathsf V$ samples $\mathbf r = (1, 2)$. The prover sends

$$\bar{\mathbf v} = 1 \cdot (2, 3) + 2 \cdot (5, 7) = (2 + 10,\; 3 + 14) = (12, 17) \equiv (1, 6) \pmod{11}.$$

To check consistency, $\mathsf V$ encodes $\bar{\mathbf v}$ and compares against an open column. Encoding:

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\bar{\mathbf v}) = (1, 6) \cdot G = (1,\; 6,\; 1 + 6,\; 1 + 12) = (1, 6, 7, 2) \pmod{11}.$$

Suppose $\mathsf V$ samples column $j = 2$ (the third position, 0-indexed). The prover opens that column: $\hat u_{1, 2} = 5$ and $\hat u_{2, 2} = 1$. $\mathsf V$ checks

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\bar{\mathbf v})_2 = 7 \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; 1 \cdot \hat u_{1, 2} + 2 \cdot \hat u_{2, 2} = 1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 1 = 7. \;\checkmark$$

One column-open per draw of $j$; repeat $\Theta(\lambda)$ times for soundness. In our toy case the codeword length is only 4, so a few opens already cover most positions — over a real Brakedown setup, $\mathsf n$ is millions but only $\Theta(\lambda)$ opens are sampled.

**Why this works (correlated agreement).** The Brakedown soundness lemma — proximity gaps for linear codes — says: if any committed word $\hat{\mathbf u}_i$ is far from every codeword, then the random combination $\sum_i r_i \cdot \hat{\mathbf u}_i$ is also far from every codeword w.h.p. over $\mathbf r$. Far-from-codeword means $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\bar{\mathbf v})$ disagrees with the linear combination on many columns, so $\Theta(\lambda)$ random columns catch the disagreement. Pass the test e.w.n.p. → every $\hat{\mathbf u}_i$ is the encoding of a unique nearby $\mathbf v_i$.

**The intuition stripped down.** A malicious prover wants to commit to *something other than* an honest encoding. Two failure modes the verifier wants to catch:

- The committed row isn't a codeword at all (just a bag of $\mathbb F_q$ values that happen to hash). Testing's column opens catch this — a non-codeword disagrees with every codeword on many positions, and any column-equation $\mathsf{Enc}(\bar{\mathbf v})_j = \sum r_i \hat u_{i,j}$ acts as a spot check that the prover's "encoding" matches what the code structure demands.
- The committed row *is* a codeword but the prover lies later about what $\mathbf v_i$ it encodes. The randomized linear combination ties everything to one cross-check: if the prover is honest about all $\mathbf v_i$, the random linear combination $\sum r_i \mathbf v_i$ and the encoded linear combination $\mathsf{Enc}(\bar{\mathbf v})$ agree everywhere; if not, they disagree on a $\delta$-fraction of positions (where $\delta$ is the code distance), so column opens catch it.

### Eval phase — "and does it give the claimed value?"

Once $\mathsf V$ trusts the rows are codewords, prove $f(\mathbf q) = y$ via the tensor identity.

1. $\mathsf P$ sends the partial sum $\bar{\mathbf v} = \sum_i \mathbf q_{1,i} \cdot \mathbf v_i^f \in \mathbb F_q^{\dim}$. Honestly, $\bar{\mathbf v} = \mathbf q_1 \cdot \mathbf v^f$ — the row-combine half of the tensor identity.
2. **Code-consistency check.** Same shape as testing: $\mathsf V$ samples $J$, opens column $j$ for each $j \in J$, checks $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\bar{\mathbf v})_j = \sum_i \mathbf q_{1,i} \cdot \hat u_{i,j}$. This pins $\bar{\mathbf v}$ to be the genuine linear combination of the committed rows.
3. **Final eval check.** $\mathsf V$ checks $y = \bar{\mathbf v} \cdot \mathbf q_2^T = \sum_i \bar v_i \cdot \mathbf q_{2,i}$. By the tensor identity, this equals $f(\mathbf q)$.

**Worked example, finishing.** Replay with $\mathbf q_1 = (9, 3)$ in place of the random $\mathbf r$.

Prover sends $\bar{\mathbf v} = \mathbf q_1 \cdot \mathbf v^f = (9, 3) \cdot \mathbf v^f = (0, 4) \pmod{11}$ — the same partial sum we computed during the tensor-identity warm-up.

Code-consistency: $\mathsf V$ encodes $\bar{\mathbf v}$ and spot-checks against a random column.

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}((0, 4)) = (0,\; 4,\; 0 + 4,\; 0 + 8) = (0, 4, 4, 8) \pmod{11}.$$

Open column $j = 0$: $\hat u_{1, 0} = 2$, $\hat u_{2, 0} = 5$. Check:

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C}(\bar{\mathbf v})_0 = 0 \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; 9 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 5 = 18 + 15 = 33 \equiv 0 \pmod{11}. \;\checkmark$$

Final eval check: $y \stackrel{?}{=} \bar{\mathbf v} \cdot \mathbf q_2^T = (0, 4) \cdot (8, 4)^T = 0 \cdot 8 + 4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$. So the verifier accepts $f(3, 4) = 5$. Matches the direct computation. ✓

The eval phase is essentially the testing phase with $\mathbf q_1$ in place of the random $\mathbf r$, plus one final inner-product check against $\mathbf q_2$.

### What survives, what changes for $\mathbb Q$

Brakedown's whole soundness story rests on **(a)** the linear code having good distance and **(b)** the correlated-agreement lemma. Over $\mathbb F_q$ both are clean — fields have nice distance theory and the lemma is well-established.

Moving to $\mathbb Q$ breaks both:

- **Distance.** Random-matrix codes don't exist over $\mathbb Q$ the way they do over $\mathbb F_q$. Building a code with good distance and _cheap encoding_ over $\mathbb Q$ is the JEA problem (§JEA).
- **Correlated agreement.** Brakedown's lemma uses field structure. Over $\mathbb Q$ we need different combinatorial tools — that's Lemmas 2.4 and 2.5 (§Why a Single Integrality Check…).
- **Bounded extraction.** A finite field bounds entry bit-size by $\log \lvert\mathbb F\rvert$ for free. Over $\mathbb Q$ nothing bounds it — the extra **integrality + size check** in Zip's testing phase is what supplies the bound.

Zip is the result of stitching these three patches into Brakedown's template.

## The Contract: Two Bit-Size Bands

Zip is a multilinear PCS for $f \in \mathbb Q_B^{\mathrm{multilin}}[\mathbf X]$ with one notable asymmetry between completeness and soundness:

|                  | Bit-size band                          | Meaning                                    |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Completeness** | $\mathbb Z_{B'}$ (integers, $B'$ bits) | Honest provers commit with this much room  |
| **Soundness**    | $\mathbb Q_B$ (rationals, $B$ bits)    | Extractor pulls back any cheat inside here |

with $B' < B$ related by

$$B = 2 \cdot B' + (6 \cdot \dim + 2) \cdot (\lambda + \log \dim),$$

where $\dim = 2^{\mu/2}$ is the side-length of the coefficient matrix (we'll see this shape in a moment).

**The IOPP-to-$\mathbb Z$ analogy.** This shape mirrors any [IOP of proximity to a code](/writing/whir#iops-of-proximity). There, the honest prover sends a real codeword, but the scheme only guarantees that a passing prover sent something _close to_ a codeword — close enough that decoding pins down a unique nearby codeword. Zip plays the same game with integers: the honest prover sends an integral polynomial in $\mathbb Z_{B'}$, but the scheme only guarantees the passing prover sent something _close to integral_ — where "close" means "rational with bounded bit-size $B$."

The slack from $B'$ to $B$ is the analog of the proximity radius in a codeword IOPP. The lemmas below set its size.

Zip is _both_ an IOPP-to-a-code (over $\mathbb Q$, via its underlying linear code) _and_ an IOPP-to-$\mathbb Z$ (via a single bit-size check in the testing phase). The two checks slot together cleanly.

## What the verifier actually sees

Before any protocol walk, anchor on what's on the wire. Across the full commit + test + eval interaction, the verifier sees:

- **At commit time.** $\dim$ Merkle roots — one per encoded row of the coefficient matrix.
- **During testing.** A single random-linear-combination vector $\bar{\mathbf v} \in \mathbb Q^{\dim}$ from the prover, plus $\Theta(\lambda)$ column openings (one Merkle path per opened column, each revealing $\dim$ entries from the committed rows).
- **During eval.** A single projected vector $\bar{\mathbf v}_q \in \mathbb F_q^{\dim}$ from the prover, plus another $\Theta(\lambda)$ column openings.

That's it. No big-integer polynomial is ever sent in the clear. The verifier's whole job is to run two consistency checks against the random-linear-combinations the prover supplies, against $\Theta(\lambda)$-sized samples of the underlying committed words.

## The Commit Phase

Same shape as Brakedown's commit phase (§Brakedown in a Nutshell). The one structural twist: encode and commit happen over $\mathbb Q$, not $\mathbb F_q$.

**Setup.** Multilinear $f(X_1, \ldots, X_\mu)$ with $\mu$ even and coefficients in $\mathbb Q_B$. Set $\dim = 2^{\mu/2}$.

**Step 1.** Arrange $f$'s coefficients on the hypercube $\{0, 1\}^\mu$ into a $\dim \times \dim$ matrix $\mathbf v^f$, each row $\mathbf v_i^f$ a length-$\dim$ vector of $\mathbb Q_B$ entries. (The tensor split derived in §Brakedown in a Nutshell.)

**Step 2.** Encode each row with the underlying linear code $\mathcal C_\lambda$ over $\mathbb Q$:

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_\lambda}(\mathbf v_i^f) \in \mathbb Q^{\mathsf n}.$$

We'll cover _which_ code in §JEA — for now, treat it as a black box mapping a $\dim$-vector to an $\mathsf n$-vector with good distance.

**Step 3.** Merkle-commit each encoded row independently:

$$\mathsf{cm} = \big(\mathsf{Commit}(\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_\lambda}(\mathbf v_i^f))\big)_{i \in [\dim]}.$$

$\dim$ separate Merkle roots. Prover work is dominated by the $\dim$ encodings; each is a $\dim$-by-$\mathsf n$ generator-matrix multiplication.

**The only structural twist vs. Brakedown.** $\mathcal C_\lambda$ is a code over $\mathbb Q$, not $\mathbb F_q$. Everything else looks identical.

## The Testing Phase

Testing is where Zip earns the "IOP of Proximity to the Integers" name. The phase is Brakedown's standard random-linear-combination check **plus one new step**: an integrality + bit-size check on the prover's response.

**Protocol walkthrough.**

1. $\mathsf V$ samples $r_1, \ldots, r_{\dim}$ uniformly from $[0, 2^\lambda - 1]$ — small $\lambda$-bit integer challenges. (The formal protocol uses $[0, q_0 - 1]$ for a fixed public prime $q_0 \approx 2^\lambda$ — a technicality that streamlines a few lemmas. For intuition, treat it as the $\lambda$-bit interval shown.)

2. $\mathsf P$ sends $\bar{\mathbf v} = \sum_{i \in [\dim]} r_i \cdot \mathbf v_i^f \in \mathbb Q^{\dim}$.

3. **(The new step.)** $\mathsf V$ rejects if any entry $\bar v_j$ is **not an integer** or has $|\bar v_j| > \dim \cdot 2^{B' + \lambda}$.

4. $\mathsf V$ samples a random subset $J \subseteq [\mathsf n]$ with $|J| = \Theta(\lambda)$. For each $j \in J$:
   - Open the Merkle commitments at column $j$, yielding $\hat u_{1,j}, \ldots, \hat u_{\dim, j}$. Reject if any $\hat u_{i,j}$ is not an integer of expected bit-size.
   - Check $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_\lambda}(\bar{\mathbf v})_j = \sum_i r_i \cdot \hat u_{i,j}$.

Steps 1, 2, 4 are Brakedown verbatim. **Step 3 is the only new ingredient.** A single line of code: "are all entries integers, and is each at most $\dim \cdot 2^{B'+\lambda}$?"

**What each step buys.** Steps 1, 2, 4 are Brakedown's correlated-agreement check — except with negligible probability (e.w.n.p.), they guarantee every $\hat{\mathbf u}_i$ is close to a unique codeword $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_\lambda}(\mathbf v_i)$. That's just "prover committed to _some_ codeword in each row."

Step 3 does the new work. It guarantees, e.w.n.p., that those underlying $\mathbf v_i$ vectors aren't just rationals but **bounded rationals**: $\mathbf v_i \in \mathbb Q_B^{\dim}$. **That's the IOPP-to-$\mathbb Z$ part of Zip.**

By the time testing passes, $\mathsf V$ knows the prover is committed to bit-size-bounded rational rows. That's exactly Lemma 2.1's precondition — and Lemma 2.1 is what the eval phase rides on top of.

## Why a Single Integrality Check Pins Bit-Size

The Step 3 check is one integer comparison. Why does it pin bit-size so cleanly? Two combinatorial lemmas about random rational linear combinations.

**Lemma 2.4 — "random linear combination of large rationals stays large".** Let $\mathbf v = (v_1, \ldots, v_n) \in \mathbb Q^n$, not all zero. Let $S = [0, 2^s - 1]$. Sample $r_i \leftarrow S$ for each $i$. Then for any $b \geq 1$,

$$\Pr\!\left[\,\Bigl|\sum_i r_i v_i\Bigr| < 2^b\,\right] \;\leq\; \min\!\left\{1, \frac{2^b}{\|\mathbf v\|_\infty \cdot 2^s}\right\}.$$

**Read:** if some $v_i$ has very large magnitude, a random small-integer combination almost certainly produces a large magnitude. The "size barrier" $\|\mathbf v\|_\infty \cdot 2^s$ scales with how large $v_i$ is.

**Lemma 2.5 — "random linear combination of fractions rarely lands on an integer".** Let $\mathbf v \in \mathbb Q^n$, not all zero, with $v_i = a_i / b_i$ in lowest form. Let $M \geq 1$, and suppose some index $i$ has both $b_i > M$ and $v_i \neq 0$ — one entry with a denominator exceeding $M$. Then

$$\Pr\!\left[\,\sum_i r_i v_i \in \mathbb Z \,\Big|\, r_i \leftarrow [0, M - 1]\,\right] \;\leq\; \frac{1}{M}.$$

**Read:** a random small-integer combination of rationals where one has a large denominator almost never lands on an integer.

### A tiny worked example

Take $n = 2$, $M = 5$ (so $r_1, r_2 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$). Consider three candidate vectors:

- **Vector A:** $\mathbf v = (1, 2)$. Both small integers. Every random combination $r_1 + 2 r_2$ is a small integer. Step 3 passes — and it should, this is the honest case.

- **Vector B:** $\mathbf v = (1, 1/7)$. One entry has denominator $7 > M = 5$. By Lemma 2.5, $\Pr[r_1 + r_2/7 \in \mathbb Z] \leq 1/5$. Concretely, $r_1 + r_2/7$ is an integer iff $7 \mid r_2$, and $r_2 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ contains only $r_2 = 0$ satisfying this — so the probability is exactly $1/5$. The cheating prover gets caught $4/5$ of the time.

- **Vector C:** $\mathbf v = (1, 1000)$. Both integers, but the second exceeds the magnitude band. By Lemma 2.4 with $\|\mathbf v\|_\infty = 1000$, sampling interval size $M = 5$ (so $s = \log_2 M$), magnitude bound $N = 1000$: $\Pr[|r_1 + 1000 r_2| < 1000] \leq 1000/(1000 \cdot 5) = 1/5$. The bound is tight here — only $r_2 = 0$ produces a small magnitude (probability $1/5$, contributing $r_1 \in [0, 4]$); any $r_2 \geq 1$ already pushes $|r_1 + 1000 r_2| \geq 996$. The cheating prover slips through $1/M$ of the time and gets caught the rest.

  Scaling intuition: as $\|\mathbf v\|_\infty$ grows past $N$, the lemma's ratio $N/(\|\mathbf v\|_\infty \cdot M)$ shrinks proportionally — but the unavoidable $r_n = 0$ slot keeps a floor near $1/M$. Zip pushes this floor to negligible by sampling $r_i$ from a $\lambda$-bit interval ($M \approx 2^\lambda$).

The two lemmas are the $\mathbb Q$-analogs of Brakedown's correlated-agreement / proximity-gap lemmas. Brakedown needs random linear combinations of "close-to-codeword" words to stay close. Zip needs random linear combinations of large rationals to stay large, and random linear combinations of fractions to rarely be integers. Same kind of combinatorial statement; different ring.

### How they combine

Step 3 demands the linear combination be both an integer _and_ small (magnitude $< \dim \cdot 2^{B'+\lambda}$). With $r_i$ drawn from $[0, 2^\lambda - 1]$:

- **Lemma 2.5** with $M = 2^\lambda$ says: if any $\mathbf v_i$ has an entry with denominator $> 2^\lambda$, the linear combination is hardly ever an integer. So passing the integrality check forces all denominators bounded by $2^\lambda$ e.w.n.p.
- **Lemma 2.4** with $s = \lambda$ says: if any $\mathbf v_i$ has an entry with magnitude $\gg 2^{B'}$, the linear combination's magnitude likely exceeds $\dim \cdot 2^{B'+\lambda}$. So passing the magnitude check forces all numerators bounded too.

Combine: passing Step 3 e.w.n.p. forces $\mathbf v_i \in \mathbb Q_B^{\dim}$ for each $i$. The slack constant $(6 \dim + 2)(\lambda + \log \dim)$ in the $B = 2 B' + \ldots$ formula absorbs the multiplicative factors hidden in the lemma-chain.

|                       | Brakedown (over $\mathbb F_q$)             | Zip (over $\mathbb Q$)                         |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Main soundness lever  | Random linear combination of close-to-codeword stays close | Random linear combination of large rationals stays large (2.4) |
| Extra lever           | (none — distance handles everything)       | Random linear combination of fractions rarely integer (2.5)    |
| Probability dominator | Field size $\lvert\mathbb F_q\rvert$       | Sampling interval $2^\lambda$                  |

## The Evaluation Phase

The eval phase proves a claim $f(\mathbf q) = y$ for a query point $\mathbf q \in \mathbb Z_B^\mu$ and value $y \in \mathbb Z$. The protocol is "Brakedown's eval, but every check happens modulo a verifier-sampled random prime."

**Protocol walkthrough.**

1. $\mathsf V$ samples a random $\Omega(\lambda)$-bit prime $q \in \mathcal P_\lambda$.

2. **Bad-denominator branch.** If any $\mathbf v_i^f$ has an entry whose denominator is divisible by $q$ — i.e., $\mathbf v_i^f \notin \mathbb Z_{(q)}^{\dim}$ — $\mathsf P$ aborts. (Same shape as Zinc-PIOP's Step 2 from [Part 1](/writing/zinc-part-1#why-its-knowledge-sound). Bounded by the same Lemma-2.1 logic: a bit-size-bounded witness can have at most $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)$ primes dividing any denominator, so this branch fires negligibly often.)

3. Otherwise, $\mathsf P$ uses $f(\mathbf q) = \mathbf q_1 \cdot \mathbf v^f \cdot \mathbf q_2^T$ with $\mathbf q_1, \mathbf q_2 \in \mathbb Z^{\dim}$ and sends the **projected partial sum**

$$\bar{\mathbf v}_q = \sum_{i \in [\dim]} \phi_q(\mathbf q_{1,i}) \cdot \phi_q(\mathbf v_i^f) \in \mathbb F_q^{\dim}.$$

4. $\mathsf V$ samples $J \subseteq [\mathsf n]$ with $|J| = \Theta(\lambda)$. For each $j \in J$:
   - Open the Merkle commitments at column $j$, yielding $\hat u_{1,j}, \ldots, \hat u_{\dim, j}$.
   - **Code-consistency check.** $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_{\lambda, q}}(\bar{\mathbf v}_q)_j = \sum_i \phi_q(\mathbf q_{1,i}) \cdot \phi_q(\hat u_{i,j})$, where $\mathcal C_{\lambda, q} = \phi_q(\mathcal C_\lambda)$ is the projected code.
   - **Final eval check.** $\phi_q(y) = \sum_i \bar v_{q, i} \cdot \phi_q(\mathbf q_{2, i})$.

**The two soundness pieces.**

The code-consistency check, by Brakedown's argument applied to the projected code $\phi_q(\mathcal C_\lambda)$, gives e.w.n.p. that $\bar{\mathbf v}_q = \sum_i \phi_q(\mathbf q_{1,i}) \cdot \phi_q(\mathbf v_i^f)$. This is why the projected code needs good distance — JEA codes are designed for exactly this (next section).

The final eval check then gives $\phi_q(y) = \phi_q(f(\mathbf q))$ — equality _modulo $q$_. We want full $\mathbb Q$-equality. To get there, **we invoke Lemma 2.1 from Part 1**. That lemma needs bit-size-bounded $\mathbf v_i$. The testing phase delivers them. The two phases interlock: testing pins entry bit-sizes; eval rides Lemma 2.1 on top of that bound.

## JEA Codes: A Linear Code That Survives Over $\mathbb Q$

Every Brakedown-style PCS rests on a code with good distance and cheap encoding. Over $\mathbb F_q$ that's solved — Brakedown's recursive code, Basefold's, Reed–Solomon. Over $\mathbb Q$, the standard tricks fail.

**Why standard codes break.** Brakedown's and Basefold's codes are built recursively via iterative matrix multiplications, with random _field_ elements at each level. Over $\mathbb F_q$, those are $\log q$ bits. Naively translating to $\mathbb Z$ — replacing field elements with integers — produces matrices whose entries blow up to thousands of bits after a few recursion levels. Encoding then needs huge-integer multiplications, defeating the whole purpose.

Reed–Solomon works over any ring, but encoding is $O(\mathsf n \log \mathsf n)$ via FFT — fine for runtime, but it kills the linear-time-prover benefit Brakedown buys you.

**JEA's answer: keep the matrix sparse with small entries.** Adapted from [BFK+24]'s expand-accumulate codes, drawing randomness from $[0, s - 1] \subset \mathbb Z$ instead of $\mathbb F_q$.

Picture two stacked sparse integer matrices: each row has only $O(\log n)$ nonzero entries, all $\sim \log s$ bits. Encoding is a sparse-matrix-vector multiply with bounded-bit numbers — no recursion, no field elements, no bit-size blowup.

### Construction (Definition 6.1)

Let $n, d \geq 1$. Let $A \in \mathbb Z^{n \times n}$ be the upper-triangular all-ones **accumulator** matrix. Let $S = [0, s - 1]$ for some integer $s > 1$.

- $E_1 \in \mathbb Z^{d \times n}$: each row is a uniformly random weight-$t$ vector with nonzero entries drawn from $S$, where $t = \gamma \log n$. ("BP" — bounded-positions.)
- $E_2 \in \mathbb Z^{d \times n}$: each entry is independently nonzero with probability $p = t/n$ (drawn from $S$ when nonzero), zero otherwise. ("Ber" — Bernoulli.)

Form $M_{\mathsf{BP}} = E_1 \cdot A$ and $M_{\mathsf{Ber}} = E_2 \cdot A$. Two linear codes over $\mathbb Q$: $\mathcal C_{\mathsf{BP}}, \mathcal C_{\mathsf{Ber}}$. The **JEA code** is their concatenation:

$$\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_{\mathsf{JEA}}}(\mathbf v) = (\mathbf v \cdot M_{\mathsf{BP}}) \,\|\, (\mathbf v \cdot M_{\mathsf{Ber}}).$$

Length $2n$, dimension $d$.

### Why this works over $\mathbb Q$

$M_{\mathsf{BP}}$ and $M_{\mathsf{Ber}}$ are sparse (because $E_1, E_2$ are), and nonzero entries have $\sim \log s$ bits. Encoding a $B'$-bit-entry integer vector costs $B'$-bit-by-$\log s$-bit multiplications and $\sim (B' + \log s)$-bit additions. No bit-size blowup. Specifically, $B' \approx 2\lambda$ and $\log s \approx 4\lambda$ are the operative numbers for Zinc — all operations stay $O(\lambda)$ bits.

### Projection requirement

The eval phase calls $\mathsf{Enc}_{\mathcal C_{\lambda, q}} = \mathsf{Enc}_{\phi_q(\mathcal C_\lambda)}$ — the code projected mod a random $q$. Brakedown's column-consistency soundness relies on the projected code having good distance.

**Theorem 6.5 (paraphrased).** As long as $s$ is large enough, the entries of $M_{\mathsf{BP}}, M_{\mathsf{Ber}}$ projected mod $q$ look uniform in $\mathbb F_q$ (e.w.n.p. over the choice of $q$).

So the projected code is essentially a JEA code over $\mathbb F_q$, which is exactly what [BFK+24] analyzed. The size threshold: per Remark 6.7, $s \approx 2^{4\lambda}$ suffices. The factor of $4$ comes from a Markov-bound chain in the projection-error analysis; Remark 6.8 conjectures it can drop to $2^{2\lambda}$ via better concentration. Operationally, $s$ controls generator-matrix bit-width — $4\lambda$ bits is fine, $2\lambda$ would be tighter.

|                       | Brakedown's recursive | Basefold's           | Reed–Solomon                  | **JEA (Zip)**                      |
| --------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Encoding speed        | Linear-time           | Linear-time          | $O(\mathsf n \log \mathsf n)$ | **Linear-time, sparse**            |
| Matrix entries        | Random $\mathbb F_q$  | Random $\mathbb F_q$ | Polynomial evals              | **$\log s \approx 4\lambda$ bits** |
| Survives $\mathbb Q$? | No — entries blow up  | No — entries blow up | Yes, but slow                 | **Yes**                            |

## The Extractor And the Expected-Polytime Wrinkle

Every PCS soundness story needs an extractor. Zip's has one quirk that doesn't show up in finite-field schemes.

**The wrinkle.** In any code-based PCS, a malicious prover can replace some entries of the committed word $\hat{\mathbf u}_i$ with arbitrary bit-strings — the Merkle tree commits to whatever bits the prover hashes in. Over a finite field $\mathbb F$, those bit-strings are at most $\log \lvert\mathbb F\rvert = O(\lambda)$ bits, so the extractor reads the entire entry at unit cost. **Over $\mathbb Q$, the prover can write an arbitrarily long bit-string into a single entry.** Reading it naively is not polynomial-time.

**The fix.** The extractor reads at most a fixed number of bits per entry — concretely

$$\log(\|M_\mathcal C\|_\infty \cdot \dim) + 2 B' + (6 \dim + 2) \log(2^\lambda \cdot \dim) \text{ bits},$$

where $\|M_\mathcal C\|_\infty$ is the largest absolute value in the JEA generator matrix. If the entry's encoding exceeds that budget, it records the position as a "$\perp$ disagreement" and moves on, instead of trying to read the rest.

**Consequence.** The extractor runs in _expected_ polynomial time, not strict polynomial time. By Lindell's witness-extended emulation (Lin01), expected-polytime extraction is equivalent to standard knowledge soundness for our purposes — the formal soundness statement still goes through.

The rest of the extractor is a standard rewinding-style PCS extractor: sample challenges $\mathbf r$, run the prover, retry on failure, build up $\dim$ linearly independent successful challenges, invert to recover the witness. Just over $\mathbb Q$, with the bit-budget cap as the one new ingredient.

## Compilation: The Pipeline

Two pieces left: turning the PIOP-plus-PCS into an IOP, then turning that IOP into a succinct argument.

**PIOP → IOP.** Replace each polynomial oracle $[[\tilde w]]$ in Zinc-PIOP with a Zip commitment — a tuple of Merkle roots over encoded coefficient-matrix rows. Replace each polynomial-oracle query in sum-check with a Zip evaluation IOP execution certifying the queried value. Result: a regular hash-based IOP, no polynomial-oracle abstraction left.

**IOP → succinct interactive argument via iCOS.** The COS transformation [COS20] compiles an IOP into a SNARK by Merkle-committing every oracle and applying Fiat–Shamir. It requires the underlying IOP to satisfy _state-restoration_ knowledge soundness. Zip provides only standard knowledge soundness (the stronger property is flagged as future work in §2.4 of the paper), so we use **iCOS** — the _interactive_ variant of COS, which skips the Fiat–Shamir step. The result is succinct _interactive_, not non-interactive.

Beneath iCOS sits **iBCS** [CDGS23], the vector-commitment-based BCS variant. Both transformations are "Merkle-commit every oracle, answer queries via Merkle openings" — succinctness comes from Merkle path length being $O(\log \mathsf n)$ rather than $\mathsf n$.

## What This Buys You

The full system, end-to-end:

- **Hash-only.** Every cryptographic primitive is collision-resistant hashing. No hidden-order groups, no RSA assumptions, no pairings. The random oracle for Fiat–Shamir is the only model dependency, standard for any hash-based SNARK.
- **Plausibly post-quantum.** Nothing in the security argument relies on group cryptography. Subject to the random oracle being instantiable, the construction has a path to PQ security.
- **Multi-modulus over $\mathbb Z$.** From Part 1's R1CSℓ structure. Zip plays no direct role here, but Zip is what _unlocks_ it by removing the hidden-order-group dependency.

**Concrete cost shape.** No benchmarks are published yet (Section 2.4 of the paper flags implementation as ongoing), so this is the asymptotic profile:

- **Commit:** $\dim$ encodings of $\dim$-length integer vectors with a sparse JEA generator matrix. Roughly $O(\dim^2 \cdot t)$ small-integer ops, where $t = \gamma \log \dim$.
- **Test:** $\Theta(\lambda)$ Merkle openings per row, plus the random-linear-combination + bit-size check.
- **Eval:** $\Theta(\lambda)$ Merkle openings, plus a $\dim$-by-$\dim$ matrix-vector product modulo a random $\Omega(\lambda)$-bit prime.
- **Bit-widths everywhere:** all operations bounded to $O(\lambda)$-bit or $O(B)$-bit integers. No thousand-bit arithmetic anywhere — that was the whole point.

Extending Part 1's headline table:

|                          | CHA24 (mod-AHP + BHR+21 PCS)        | **Zinc + Zip**                    |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| PCS family               | Hidden-order group                  | Brakedown (hash + linear code)    |
| Cryptographic assumption | RSA-style (groups of unknown order) | Collision-resistant hashing + ROM |
| Post-quantum             | No                                  | Plausibly yes                     |
| Multi-modulus per stmt   | Yes                                 | Yes                               |
| Arithmetization overhead | None                                | None                              |
| Underlying linear code   | n/a                                 | JEA (sparse, small-entry)         |

## Conclusion

Five core ideas drive Zinc + Zip:

1. **A Brakedown-style PCS over $\mathbb Q$ is possible** if you're careful about what the prover commits to, what the testing phase proves, and what code you use.
2. **The IOPP-to-$\mathbb Z$ abstraction** — "prover knows a word close to integral" — slots into Brakedown's existing IOPP-to-code abstraction. Zip is both at once.
3. **Two combinatorial lemmas (2.4, 2.5)** replace Brakedown's correlated-agreement bounds in the $\mathbb Q$ setting. Easier to state than Brakedown's, in fact.
4. **JEA codes** — sparse generator matrix, small entries — are a linear code that survives translation from $\mathbb F_q$ to $\mathbb Q$ without bit-size blowup. Most other linear codes don't.
5. **iCOS** is the standard Merkle compilation step. Fiat–Shamir for non-interactivity is open work.

What makes Zinc + Zip significant: it's the first **hash-only succinct argument for native integer-arithmetic statements**, with multi-modulus support at zero arithmetization overhead, and a plausibly post-quantum path. The construction recipe — "hash + linear code over $\mathbb Q$" — is clean, and unlocks a class of relations that previously required RSA-style assumptions to handle efficiently.

Open theoretical question: sharpen the JEA projection-error analysis to drop $s$ from $2^{4\lambda}$ to $2^{2\lambda}$ (Remark 6.8) and shave constants. Open practical question: implementation — benchmarks against Brakedown over $\mathbb F_q$ will show whether the bit-bookkeeping overhead is small enough in practice for the integer-arithmetic flexibility to pay off.
